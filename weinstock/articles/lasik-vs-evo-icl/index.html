LASIK vs. EVO ICL: How to Know Which Fits You

Both can free you from glasses and contacts, but they work in very different ways. Here's how to tell which one fits your eyes, your prescription and your life.

Key takeaways
  • LASIK uses a laser to reshape the cornea and is FDA approved to treat nearsightedness, farsightedness and astigmatism.
  • EVO ICL is a thin lens placed inside the eye, behind the iris, without removing corneal tissue.
  • EVO ICL is often considered for higher prescriptions, thinner corneas or dry eye concerns.
  • Neither procedure prevents the need for reading glasses as you age; lens-based options may fit better after about 45.

How each procedure works

LASIK uses a laser to reshape the cornea, the clear front window of the eye, so light focuses correctly. LASIK has been approved by the FDA to treat nearsightedness, farsightedness and astigmatism.1

EVO ICL (Implantable Collamer Lens) is a thin lens placed inside the eye, behind the iris, in front of your natural lens. Unlike LASIK, it corrects vision without removing corneal tissue.2 Because your natural lens stays in place, the approach is described as reversible and keeps future treatment options open.3

The FDA approved the EVO ICL in the United States on March 25, 2022, for correcting nearsightedness and nearsightedness with astigmatism.4 Earlier versions of the lens required a small laser opening in the iris before surgery; the EVO design does not.5

Who tends to fit LASIK

LASIK is often a strong option for adults whose prescription has been stable and whose corneas are healthy and thick enough to reshape. A thorough baseline evaluation decides whether you are a good candidate.6

Dry eye matters. The FDA notes that existing dry eye can get worse after LASIK, and some people develop dry eye as a result of surgery, so you should be screened before treatment.7

Who tends to fit EVO ICL

EVO ICL is often considered when LASIK is not the best fit: higher amounts of nearsightedness, corneas that are thin or irregular, or dry eye that makes corneal surgery less appealing. At FDA approval it was indicated for adults 21 to 45,8 and the FDA has since approved an expanded age indication.3

What if you're over 45?

Around your mid-40s, the eye's natural lens starts losing its ability to focus up close, a condition called presbyopia. Neither LASIK nor ICL stops that, and the FDA notes that people who used reading glasses before LASIK may still need them afterward.7

For many people in this stage, a lens-based procedure such as Clear Lens Exchange can address near and distance vision together and remove the chance of a future cataract. See Clear Lens Exchange in Largo.

Questions to ask your surgeon

  • Which procedure do my measurements support, and why?
  • How many of these procedures have you performed with this laser or lens?7
  • Will I still need reading glasses, now or later?
  • What does recovery look like, and how many follow-up visits will I have?

After LASIK, expect to see your doctor within the first 24 to 48 hours and at regular visits after that.6

Frequently asked questions

Is EVO ICL better than LASIK?

Neither is better for everyone. LASIK is a strong option for many people with stable prescriptions and healthy corneas, while EVO ICL is often a better fit for higher prescriptions, thin corneas or dry eye concerns.

Can EVO ICL be removed?

Yes. Because the lens is placed inside the eye without removing corneal tissue, it can be removed or exchanged if needed.

Will I need reading glasses after LASIK or ICL?

Possibly. Both procedures correct distance vision but do not stop presbyopia, the age-related loss of near focus that usually begins in the mid-40s.

How do I know which one I qualify for?

A full evaluation measures your prescription, corneal thickness and shape, and the internal dimensions of your eye. Those measurements determine which procedure is safe and effective for you.
